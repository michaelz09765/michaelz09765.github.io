Q 1.1 A Year of Losses profit · expenses · reinforces real Q2
THE QUESTION · 5 MARKS

GHI had revenue of 120,000 and a loss of 15,000 in 2026. Its total expenses were:

  1. 105,000
  2. 15,000
  3. 135,000
  4. 120,000
Answer(c) 135,000
The big idea: profit is what revenue leaves behind

Profit is revenue minus expenses, so a loss means expenses ran past revenue.

  • Reinforces real midterm Q2 (“revenues are always bigger than profits”): the same equation, read backwards.
Definition
Profit and Loss
$$\text{Profit} = \text{Revenue} - \text{Expenses}$$
IN PLAIN WORDS
  • Profit is the slice of revenue left after you pay every cost of earning it.
  • A loss is a negative profit: a loss of 15,000 means profit $= -15{,}000$.

Write the equation with the numbers in, $-15{,}000 = 120{,}000 - E$, and solve for $E$ before you read on.

Answer
(c) 135,000
$E = \text{Revenue} - \text{Profit} = 120{,}000 - (-15{,}000) = 135{,}000$
  • c. 135,000. Expenses beat revenue by exactly the loss.
  • a. 105,000. Treats the loss as a profit of 15,000. That company would have made money.
  • b. 15,000. The size of the loss, the gap between the two figures.
  • d. 120,000. Expenses equal to revenue give a profit of zero, not a loss.
The intuition: a lemonade stand in the rain

You sell 120£ of lemonade, but lemons, cups and the pitch fee cost 135£. You end the day 15£ down.

Costs that revenue covers Costs nobody pays for: the loss
Fig 1.1 · Expenses run 15,000 past revenue
  • Back to real Q2: with expenses above zero, profit is always smaller than revenue, and a loss only widens the gap.
AnswerExpenses $= 120{,}000 - 15{,}000 = 105{,}000$: option a.

Q 1.3 The Van in Year Three reducing balance · reinforces real Q9
THE QUESTION · 5 MARKS

Company JKL bought a van for 50,000 on 1 January 2024. It depreciates the van using the reducing balance method at 30% a year; its year ends on 31 December. The depreciation charge for 2026 is:

  1. 15,000
  2. 7,350
  3. 17,150
  4. 10,500
Answer(b) 7,350
The big idea: the rate bites on what is left

Reducing balance charges 30% of the NBV at the start of each year, so walk the van through 2024 and 2025 first: each charge takes 30% of a smaller number.

  • Reinforces real midterm Q9 (closing NBV 153,600): same method; here you stop at the charge.
Definition
Reducing Balance Depreciation
$$\text{Charge}_t = r \times \text{NBV}_{\text{start of } t}, \qquad \text{NBV} = \text{cost} - \text{accumulated depreciation}$$
IN PLAIN WORDS
  • NBV (net book value) is what the books still say the asset is worth.
  • Each year you take 30% of that and keep 70%, so the NBV goes $\times 0.7$ every year.

(b) 7,350
$0.30 \times 50{,}000 \times 0.7^2 = 0.30 \times 24{,}500 = 7{,}350$
  • b. 7,350. 30% of the NBV at the start of 2026.
  • a. 15,000. 30% of cost. That is the 2024 charge, and charging it every year is straight-line thinking.
  • c. 17,150. The NBV at the end of 2026: what is left, not what was used up.
  • d. 10,500. The 2025 charge: one year too early.

Q 1.5 Rent Paid in Cash accruals · cash against expense
THE QUESTION · 5 MARKS

Company DEF’s rent expense for 2026 is 6,500. Accrued rent was 500 at the end of 2025 and 700 at the end of 2026. How much rent did DEF pay in cash in 2026?

  1. 6,300
  2. 6,700
  3. 7,700
  4. 5,300
Answer(a) 6,300
The big idea: cash and expense count different months

Cash paid in 2026 includes last year’s unpaid bill and misses this year’s unpaid one. The question gives you the expense and asks for the cash, so you run the accrual rule backwards.

The trap
Why 6,700 Looks Right

6,700 is the accrual formula you learned for real Q4, with the question’s three numbers dropped in: $6{,}500 - 500 + 700$.

  • It feels safe: same formula, same kind of numbers, an answer near 6,500.
  • Where it breaks: that formula turns cash paid into expense. Here 6,500 is already the expense, so you put it in the “paid” slot, and the formula moves it the wrong way.
  • The check exposes it: if DEF had paid 6,700, its expense would be $6{,}700 - 500 + 700 = 6{,}900$, not the 6,500 the question states.
  • $\text{Paid} = 6{,}500 - 500 + 700 = 6{,}700$: the expense formula, run backwards.
  • $\text{Paid} = \text{Expense} + \text{opening} - \text{closing} = 6{,}500 + 500 - 700 = 6{,}300$.
Definition
Accrual
$$\text{Expense} = \text{Paid} - \text{opening accrual} + \text{closing accrual}$$
“Expenses are recorded when incurred, regardless of when cash is paid” (Lecture 2). An accrual is an expense incurred but not yet paid: a liability.
IN PLAIN WORDS
  • Opening accrual (500): last year’s rent you paid this year. Cash, yes; this year’s cost, no: take it out.
  • Closing accrual (700): this year’s rent you have not paid yet. Cost, yes; cash, no: add it.
  • Backwards (expense to cash): both signs flip, $\text{Paid} = \text{Expense} + \text{opening} - \text{closing}$.

Before any formula, fill the table below: which of the three pieces of rent did DEF pay for with 2026 cash?

Working
The Method From Scratch
1Split the rent into three pieces by when it was used and when it was paid.
2December 2025 rent (500), paid January 2026: 2026 cash, not 2026 expense.
3December 2026 rent (700), unpaid at 31 December: 2026 expense, not cash.
4The rest of 2026’s rent is both: $6{,}500 - 700 = 5{,}800$.
5Add each column: cash 6,300; expense 6,500 ✓

The table works in either direction, so you never need to remember which way the signs go.
Answer
(a) 6,300
$\text{Paid} = 6{,}500 + 500 - 700 = 6{,}300$; check: $6{,}300 - 500 + 700 = 6{,}500$ ✓
  • a. 6,300. The expense formula, solved for cash.
  • b. 6,700. Uses the expense formula the wrong way round: $6{,}500 - 500 + 700$.
  • c. 7,700. Adds both accruals, as if DEF also paid next year’s bill.
  • d. 5,300. Takes both away, so it misses the 500 paid for 2025.

Q 1.6 Same Machine, Two Charges standards · reinforces real Q7
THE QUESTION · 5 MARKS

Two companies buy identical machines on the same day, and both prepare their accounts under IFRS. Which statement is correct?

  1. They must report the same depreciation charge: IFRS sets one rule for each transaction
  2. They may report different depreciation charges, because IFRS lets each company choose its method
  3. They must both use straight-line depreciation, the only method IFRS allows
  4. None of the answers is correct
Answer(b) Different charges, by choice
The big idea: rules with options

Standards fix what you must report, but often let each company pick between methods the rules allow.

  • Reinforces real midterm Q7 (“accounting standards leave choices to companies”): here you see the choice at work.
Definition
Accounting Standards
“Accounting standards: technical rules that regulate how specific events should be accounted for and how the financial statements should be presented. … Sometimes they provide different options for accounting the same transaction: the entity can choose.” (Lecture 2)
IN PLAIN WORDS
  • The rule: every machine must be depreciated (IAS 16).
  • The choice: straight line or reducing balance, and the useful life the company expects.

Name two choices IAS 16 gives a company before you read the options again.

Answer
(b) Different Charges Are Allowed
  • b. Each company picks its method, so two IFRS reporters can show different charges for identical machines.
  • a. IFRS does not set one rule for each transaction; the lecture says it sometimes offers options.
  • c. IFRS allows reducing balance too: the lecture covers both methods (Q1.9).
  • d. None correct. b is correct.
The intuition: one 10,000 machine, two first-year charges
Fig 1.4 · Year-1 depreciation under each choice
  • Firm X: straight line over five years, $10{,}000 / 5 = 2{,}000$. Firm Y: reducing balance at 40%, $0.4 \times 10{,}000 = 4{,}000$.
  • Both follow IFRS. Firm Y reports 2,000 less profit in year 1, and neither firm has broken a rule.
Exam pitfall: the real Q7 distractors
  • Standards are the same worldwide, and independent from legislation.
  • The US uses US GAAP, not IFRS; the UK Companies Act 2006 backs the standards in law (Lecture 2: “who sets the accounting rules?”).
AnswerDifferent rule books as well as different choices: their figures can differ for both reasons.
Q 1.7 A Machine Sold Cheap disposals · reinforces real Q16
THE QUESTION · 5 MARKS

Company MNO sells for £9,000 in cash a machine that cost £60,000 and has accumulated depreciation of £45,000. The effect on the financial statements is:

  1. Cash increases by 9,000; PPE is reduced by 60,000; Profit decreases by 51,000
  2. Cash increases by 9,000; PPE is reduced by 15,000; Profit increases by 9,000
  3. Cash increases by 9,000; PPE is reduced by 9,000; no effect on profit
  4. Cash increases by 9,000; PPE is reduced by 15,000; Profit decreases by 6,000
Answer(d) Cash +9,000; PPE −15,000; profit −6,000
The big idea: a sale removes the book value

You give up the machine at its NBV and get the price in cash; profit moves by the difference, here a loss.

  • Reinforces real midterm Q16 (a gain of 60,000): the same three effects, with the price below NBV.
Definition
Disposal of PPE
$$\text{Gain or loss} = \text{sale price} - \text{NBV}$$
“If the sale price is above the net book value the business records a gain … if the sale price is less than the net book value you made a loss.” (Lecture 6)
IN PLAIN WORDSBoth the cost (60,000) and its accumulated depreciation (45,000) leave the books, so net PPE falls by the NBV, 15,000.

Find the NBV first, then compare it with the 9,000 price.

Answer
(d) A Loss of 6,000
1NBV $= 60{,}000 - 45{,}000 = 15{,}000$
2Loss $= 9{,}000 - 15{,}000 = -6{,}000$
3Check: assets $+9{,}000 - 15{,}000 = -6{,}000$, equal to the fall in profit
  • d. Cash +9,000, PPE down by the NBV, profit down 6,000.
  • a. Removes the cost and forgets the 45,000 of depreciation leaves too: $9{,}000 - 60{,}000$.
  • b. Books the whole price as profit, as if the machine cost nothing.
  • c. PPE falls by the NBV, not by the price; the two differ, so profit moves.
The intuition: selling your bike for less than your books say
Cash you get back What you lose
Fig 1.5 · The 15,000 NBV against the 9,000 price
  • You hand over something your books value at 15,000 and get 9,000 back: 6,000 worse off.
AnswerGain $= 20{,}000 - 15{,}000 = 5{,}000$; PPE still falls by 15,000.

Q 1.9 How Reducing Balance Behaves accelerated depreciation · reinforces real Q5
THE QUESTION · 5 MARKS

Reducing balance depreciation:

  1. Applies the same percentage to the asset’s original cost every year
  2. Gives a smaller charge each year than the year before
  3. Gives its largest charges at the end of the asset’s life
  4. All of the answers are correct
Answer(b) A smaller charge each year
The big idea: test each statement before “all of the answers”

A fixed rate on a shrinking NBV gives shrinking charges. Only b survives; this time “all of the answers” is the trap.

  • Reinforces real midterm Q5, where all three statements held. Here two of them are false, so the same option loses.
Definition
Two Methods From Lecture 6
“Straight line: most common method, constant depreciation over time. Reducing balance: accelerated depreciation method, loss of value is highest in the first year and it reduces every year.”
IN PLAIN WORDSAccelerated means bigger charges early than straight line would give, so the asset’s value falls fastest when it is new.

Write the Q1.3 van’s first three charges and check each statement against them.

Answer
(b) A Smaller Charge Each Year
$\text{Charge}_{t+1} = r \times \text{NBV}_t (1 - r) = (1 - r)\,\text{Charge}_t < \text{Charge}_t$
  • b. Smaller each year. Each charge is 70% of the one before at a 30% rate: 15,000, 10,500, 7,350.
  • a. Rate on original cost. That gives the same charge every year: straight line. Reducing balance uses the opening NBV.
  • c. Largest charges at the end. The reverse: the largest charge comes in year 1.
  • d. All correct. a and c are false.
The intuition: the van’s charges, year by year

Five years on the Q1.3 van, against straight line to the same value.

  • Reducing balance starts at 15,000 and halves in about two years.
  • Straight line charges 8,319 every year.

Q 1.10 When Going Concern Fails concepts · reinforces real Q15
THE QUESTION · 5 MARKS

If a business is no longer a going concern, its assets should be measured at:

  1. Historical cost
  2. Historical cost minus accumulated depreciation, as usual
  3. None of the answers is correct
  4. Their net realisable value: what the business could get by selling them today
Answer(d) Net realisable value
The big idea: no future, no book value

Book values assume the business keeps using its assets. If it is closing, an asset is worth only what a buyer pays today.

  • Reinforces real midterm Q15 (going concern means the company continues trading normally): this is the other side of that rule.
Definition
Going Concern
“The going concern concept is the idea that the entity will continue operating for the foreseeable future. … If the business is not likely to continue trading, assets and values should be determined using their net realisable value (what the business could earn today by selling them).” (Lecture 2)
IN PLAIN WORDSGoing concern is the normal case: the business carries on, so normal rules apply. Sainsbury’s directors confirm it each year for the next twelve months.

Picture a closing-down sale: what price does each machine fetch?

Answer
(d) Net Realisable Value
  • d. Net realisable value. The lecture’s own words for a business that will not keep trading.
  • b. Cost minus depreciation, as usual. “As usual” is the going-concern rule, the one that no longer holds.
  • a. Historical cost. Ignores both depreciation and the closure.
  • c. None correct. d is correct.
The intuition: a delivery van on the last day
Book value, still in use What a buyer pays today Value lost on closure
Fig 1.8 · One van, two bases
  • Depreciation spreads cost over future years. With no future years, that spreading means nothing, and the van drops to its 7,000 resale price.

Q 1.11 Rent Paid in Advance prepayments · matching
THE QUESTION · 5 MARKS

On 1 October 2026, STU Ltd pays 3,600 for twelve months of office rent in advance. The effect on the financial statements for the year to 31 December 2026 is:

  1. A cost of 3,600 in the income statement
  2. A cost of 900 in the income statement, an accrual of 2,700 in the balance sheet
  3. A cost of 900 in the income statement, a prepayment of 2,700 in the balance sheet
  4. A cost of 2,700 in the income statement, a prepayment of 900 in the balance sheet
Answer(c) Cost 900; prepayment 2,700
The big idea: pay now, use later

Only three of the twelve months fall in 2026. The other nine are paid for but not yet used: an asset.

Definition
Prepayment
$$\text{Expense} = \text{Paid} \times \frac{\text{months used this year}}{\text{months paid for}}, \qquad \text{Prepayment} = \text{Paid} - \text{Expense}$$
IN PLAIN WORDSA prepayment is cash paid for a later period. It is an asset (the right to use the office later), the mirror image of an accrual.

Count the months from 1 October to 31 December before you divide.

Answer
(c) Cost 900, Prepayment 2,700
$3{,}600 \times \tfrac{3}{12} = 900$ in the income statement; $3{,}600 - 900 = 2{,}700$ in the balance sheet
  • c. Three months of cost; nine months prepaid.
  • a. 3,600. Charges the cash paid, ignoring that most of it covers 2027.
  • b. An accrual. An accrual is a cost used but unpaid. Here STU paid first and uses later.
  • d. Swaps the two figures: nine months used in three months of 2026 is impossible.
The intuition: a gym membership paid up front
Used in 2026: cost Still to come: prepayment
Fig 1.9 · The 3,600 month by month
  • In 2027 the 2,700 prepayment becomes that year’s rent cost, month by month, until 30 September.
AnswerSix months each side: cost 1,800, prepayment 1,800.
Q 1.12 Reading a T Account ledgers · reinforces real Q10
THE QUESTION · 5 MARKS

Which of the following statements about T accounts is correct?

  1. Every T account has equal debit and credit totals before a balance is carried down
  2. T accounts are used under IFRS but not under UK or US GAAP
  3. The balance of a T account is the difference between the totals of its debit side and its credit side
  4. Each transaction is recorded on both sides of the same T account
Answer(c) Balance = the gap between the sides
The big idea: the gap is the information

A T account’s two sides almost never match. The difference, its balance, is the number that goes to the trial balance.

  • Reinforces real midterm Q10 (T accounts are common to all accounting systems): the same two traps, “always balanced” and “only in IAS”, come back here.
Definition
